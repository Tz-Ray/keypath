A puzzle cipher

Hide a message in the keys you'd press to type it.

KeyPath turns a message into the keystrokes you would type for it on a Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Vietnamese or Greek keyboard, or in a Spanish accent-digit scheme. A small key records every choice on the way, so the walk back is exact.

A puzzle, not a lock: it's built to be solved, not to keep secrets.

How it works

    
    
      
  1. 
        1
        
    Look it up.
    
        
    A word can hop into the keyboard's language through a public dictionary, always by way of English. The key notes which sense was used.
    
        
    
          welcome
          →
          歡迎
          sense 2 of 2

  2. 
        2
        
    Sound it out.
    
        
    Each character is spelled by its sound (on Cangjie and Quick, by its shape), and many share one: 46 characters are read ㄧㄥˊ (yíng). The key notes which one was meant.
    
        
    
          ㄧㄥˊ
          →
          營迎螢盈…
          2nd of 46
        
    
      
    3. 
      
  3. 
        3
        
    Press the keys.
    
        
    The sound is typed on a real keyboard layout: on Bopomofo, u is ㄧ, / is ㄥ and 6 is the rising tone. The keystrokes are the whole ciphertext.
    
        
    
          
            uㄧ/ㄥ6ˊ
          
          →
          cj0u/6ru8
        
    
      
    4. 
      
  4. 
        4
        
    Walk back.
    
        
    With the key, decoding is a chain of lookups, exact to the byte. Without it, a solver has to recognise the keyboard and choose characters that make sense. That's the puzzle.
    
        
    
          cj0u/6ru8 + { key }
          →
          welcome home

One line can switch keyboards mid-stream: sono3gatsu1andthesun2k7s84g4 is “la luna y el sol de la ciudad” on Japanese romaji, English and Bopomofo. The digits change meaning at every switch.

    

  






  

    

      
A puzzle, not a lock

      
KeyPath hides a message in plain sight, the way a crossword hides a word. Its dictionaries are public and its keyboard layouts are published, so it is not encryption and offers no security: like a book cipher, all it keeps back is the route. A short message is often solvable without the key, which is the fun of it. Don't use it to protect anything.

Twelve puzzles to crack

    
No key this time. Work out the keyboard, read the keys, and pick the words that make sense. Each set of six starts easy, and from #7 on, three hints wait on each card, one per click. The workbench below reads keys on the keyboard you name. Difficulty labels are the setter's intent: in playtests, AI solvers that read the languages found most of #7 to #12 easier than labelled.

    

      
    

    
All keyboards are PC layouts. Answers ignore spaces, punctuation and capitals; Chinese characters may be Traditional or Simplified, and numbers may be written as digits. Each puzzle, with its answer and a write-up of how to solve it (both spoilers), is also in the project's puzzles folder.

  






  

    
Workbench

    
For solving by hand. Name the keyboard you think the keys were typed on, then look up the keys one unit at a time, or type a guess to see the keys it makes. The workbench never guesses the keyboard or where the units split: that part of the puzzle is yours.

Look up keys

          

            
            
          

          
A unit is the keys of one character (of one syllable in Korean and Vietnamese, of one word in Russian, Greek, Spanish and English), for example cj0 u/6 ru8 on Bopomofo. For each unit you get its reading and its candidates, numbered from 0 as a key numbers them, or the rule it breaks.

          

            

              
              
            

            
          

          

          

        

        

          
Type a guess

          

            
            
          

          
Each line is a unit, its keys and which candidate it is, counting from 0: 3/62 is the fourth of 62. Text this keyboard can't type is shown as a literal.
